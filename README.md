Physics the New Religion
Michael Biswell
In Time Past
In earlier centuries, philosophers and natural thinkers rarely stood completely apart from the belief systems of their age. Plato and Aristotle reasoned within a Greek world of temples, gods, and civic ritual; medieval scholars such as Thomas Aquinas worked just inside the boundaries of Church authority; Islamic thinkers such as Avicenna and Averroes pursued medicine, mathematics, and metaphysics within cultures shaped by faith. Yet not every questioning mind could speak openly. Some survived by making their arguments appear orthodox, placing dangerous ideas behind acceptable language, religious references, or commentary on older authorities. Others lived almost like secret philosophers. Spinoza published cautiously, exchanged ideas through private correspondence, and left his most radical work to appear after his death. Descartes delayed publishing parts of his natural philosophy after seeing what happened to Galileo. Galileo himself wrapped argument in dialogue and caution yet still faced the power of religious judgement. Giordano Bruno paid the highest price for refusing to withdraw ideas that challenged the accepted order. Private notebooks, trusted circles, anonymous texts, delayed publication, and coded language became ways of keeping thought alive when public truth was dangerous. The boundary between wisdom and heresy was often narrow, and to cross it too openly could mean exile, imprisonment, professional ruin, or worse. Even Newton moved between mathematics, theology, alchemy, and the search for divine order, revealing how closely knowledge and forbidden inquiry could overlap. Belief gave society an anchor, a fixed point from which questioning minds could examine existence, but that anchor also marked the limits beyond which intellectual danger began.
What bound these worlds together was not ignorance, but orthodoxy. Each age protected an accepted picture of existence: a universe ordered by divine purpose, guarded by institutions, rituals, and authorities who decided which questions could be asked safely. Dogma gave that order its language, while heresy named the danger of stepping beyond it. One thinker might be tolerated if his arguments could be folded back into accepted belief; another might be silenced if the same questions appeared to loosen the foundations of power. The Church, the academy, the court, and the learned society all played their part in deciding where thought ended and offence began. In this way, the past was not simply a battle between religion and reason, but a struggle over who had the authority to define truth.
Today’s World
The stake has changed, but the demand for obedience has not disappeared. In the religious age, refusal to bend could cost a thinker his life, his freedom, or his place in society. In today’s world, the punishment is usually less visible, but it can still be ruinous. A scientist who questions the accepted structure may not face fire, prison, or a charge of heresy, but he may lose funding, reputation, publication, position, and the right to be heard. The suffering may appear less brutal, yet in a world where identity and livelihood are tied to institutional approval, professional exile can still become a kind of destruction. This is where physics begins to resemble a new religion: it has an orthodoxy, it has dogma, and its universities can start to look like seminaries where true believers are ordained, and doubters are quietly pushed outside the gate.
Einstein gives the better example of how science should behave. General relativity was not accepted merely because it was elegant, revolutionary, or mathematically powerful; it still had to face the sky itself. In 1919, Arthur Eddington’s eclipse observations appeared to confirm Einstein’s prediction that the Sun’s gravity would bend the light of distant stars. Only then did the theory gain wider public and scientific authority. Even one of the greatest ideas in physics had to submit to evidence rather than demand belief, and that is the standard any modern theory should still be willing to meet.
So many theories have been put forward to explain existence and being, yet not all of them stand on the same ground of proof. String theory promises a hidden structure beneath reality, with vibrating strings, extra dimensions, and possible unification of all forces, but after decades it still struggles to produce clear experimental confirmation. The multiverse offers an even grander vision: not one universe, but countless universes, perhaps with different laws, constants, and histories. Inflation explains the early expansion of the cosmos and has real observational support, yet some versions drift toward eternal inflation and endless unseen worlds. Dark matter is treated as necessary because galaxies behave as if unseen mass is there, but the thing itself remains undetected in any final, direct way. Supersymmetry was expected by many to appear in particle experiments, yet its absence has not ended the belief in it; instead, the explanation is often moved further out of reach. These ideas may be mathematically elegant, and some may yet prove true, but they also show how modern physics can ask for patience, trust, and continued funding long after ordinary proof has failed to arrive.
Other controversies show the same tension between evidence, interpretation, and institutional loyalty. The debate between dark matter and modified gravity, such as MOND, is not simply a technical disagreement about galaxies; it is a disagreement over what kind of unseen thing science is allowed to believe in. Dark matter preserves the existing framework by adding invisible mass, while MOND asks whether gravity itself has been misunderstood at galactic scales. The Hubble tension raises another difficulty, because different methods of measuring the expansion of the universe do not agree as neatly as the standard model would like. Quantum mechanics still carries its own unresolved mysteries, especially the measurement problem: whether reality has definite properties before observation, and what the act of measurement actually means. The cosmological constant problem is perhaps even more embarrassing, because the predicted energy of empty space and the observed value differ by an almost absurd scale. Quantum gravity remains unfinished, with loop quantum gravity, causal sets, emergent spacetime, and other approaches competing for authority without decisive experimental victory. Each controversy is different, and none should be dismissed lightly, but together they reveal a field in which interpretation can harden into allegiance before nature has delivered a final verdict.
The problem of measurement makes the issue even sharper. In ordinary science, measurement is supposed to reveal what is there, but in quantum mechanics the act of measurement appears to help decide what becomes definite. A particle may be described as existing in a spread of possible states until observation forces one result to appear. Different interpretations try to explain this in different ways: the Copenhagen view asks us to accept collapse, many-worlds multiplies reality into branching outcomes, hidden-variable theories search for a deeper mechanism, and relational or information-based approaches change what is meant by a physical state at all. Each interpretation can fit much of the same experimental success, yet they tell very different stories about reality. Here again the danger is not that physicists argue over mystery, because mystery is where discovery begins. The danger is when one interpretation gains the protection of habit, authority, or fashion, and students are taught to repeat it as though it were the voice of nature itself rather than one possible reading of what measurement shows. When the means of measurement becomes more than passive observation, modern physics must also ask whether its instruments are merely discovering reality or helping to produce the version of reality it then claims as proof.
The problem is not that these theories exist. Science needs imagination, and every great discovery begins before it is fully proven. The problem begins when imagination becomes protected belief. A young physicist who questions the usefulness of a fashionable theory may find that the path to grants, posts, conferences, and publication runs through the very ideas he doubts. To belong, he must learn the language, cite the approved names, and work inside the accepted frame. The old priesthood wore robes and guarded scripture; the new priesthood may wear conference badges, hold university chairs, control journals, and decide which questions count as serious. This does not make every physicist corrupt or every theory false, but it does show how easily a living search for truth can become a structure of obedience.
Einstein himself was not free from assumed belief. When he applied general relativity to the universe as a whole, he expected a static cosmos, one that endured rather than expanded or collapsed. To preserve that expectation, he introduced the cosmological constant into his equations, a mathematical pressure against gravity that allowed the universe to remain still. Later, the work of astronomers such as Edwin Hubble showed that distant galaxies were receding, and the static universe could no longer stand as comfortably as before. Einstein had to abandon his preference and accept that reality had answered against him. The importance of the episode is not that Einstein was wrong, but that evidence still had the authority to correct him. A belief, even in the mind of a genius, had to give way when the universe answered back.
This is where unquestionable inquiry begins to appear. A theory may be presented as provisional in principle, yet protected in practice because careers, departments, instruments, grants, and reputations have grown around it. String theory can retreat into higher energies or extra dimensions beyond present experiment. Supersymmetry can survive non-discovery by moving its particles beyond the range of current machines. Dark matter can remain central even after repeated detector failures because the wider cosmological model depends on it. Inflation can split into versions that explain almost any outcome, making it difficult to say what result would finally count against it. Even multiverse thinking can place explanation beyond observation altogether, asking us to accept that what cannot be tested may still be scientifically meaningful. In each case, the danger is not inquiry itself, but inquiry becoming insulated from failure: no clear point at which the theory must yield, no agreed requirement that would force retreat, and no willingness to ask what continued belief is meant to achieve.
When scientific fact cannot yet be supplied, people are often expected to accept these theories on the basis of confidence, authority, mathematical elegance, or institutional reputation rather than falsification or demonstrated fact. This is where the danger begins. A theory may be useful as a question, a model, or a possibility, but when it becomes something that cannot be allowed to fail, it steps outside the discipline of science and begins to resemble doctrine.
So much of physics today still rests on faith in frameworks that are not always made open to failure, and that is where science risks becoming bad science.

Part 2
The Economics of Inquiry: Power Structures and the Future of Scientific Freedom

Modern scientific research is shaped not only by evidence and theory but by the institutional, economic, and cultural forces that determine which questions are pursued, which theories are protected, and which lines of inquiry are discouraged. This paper examines the structural influences that govern contemporary physics, focusing on the roles of military funding, societal priorities, and academic incentives. It explores how these forces shape entire fields, how they create intellectual orthodoxy, and how they render certain theories “too big to fail.” The analysis concludes with a framework for scientific renewal, outlining principles necessary to restore scientific freedom and ensure that inquiry remains guided by truth rather than institutional survival.
1. Introduction
Scientific inquiry has never existed in isolation. Throughout history, the pursuit of knowledge has been shaped by the institutions that support it and the constraints that limit it. In earlier eras, religious authority defined the boundaries of acceptable thought; today, economic structures, institutional incentives, and cultural expectations play a similar role. The moral and intellectual pressures described in earlier critiques of scientific orthodoxy are inseparable from the financial and institutional systems that sustain modern research.
Physics, in particular, has become an intricate ecosystem in which certain theories support entire infrastructures of funding, collaboration, and academic identity. As a result, some ideas cannot be allowed to fail without destabilizing the system built around them. This paper examines the forces that shape this ecosystem, the consequences for scientific inquiry, and the conditions necessary for a future in which science can once again pursue truth without structural constraint.
2. Power Structures Behind Inquiry
Scientific research is influenced by three dominant institutional forces: the military, society, and the university. Each exerts pressure on the direction of inquiry, shaping which questions are asked, which theories are supported, and which intellectual risks are discouraged.


2.1 The Military: Capability as Priority
The military funds research that yields strategic advantage. Its interest lies in technologies that improve detection, communication, navigation, modelling, and weaponry. Fields such as nuclear physics, quantum sensing, materials science, and aerospace engineering flourish under this influence. Foundational or philosophical questions, by contrast, receive little support because they do not produce immediate capability.
The pattern is not abstract. The Manhattan Project showed how military urgency could concentrate money, talent, laboratories, and political authority around a single scientific objective. Later, Cold War investment in radar, nuclear weapons, satellites, computing, and materials research helped define which areas of physics appeared nationally essential. Such funding did not make the science false; it made certain questions powerful because they served strategic necessity.
2.2 Society: Urgency and Prestige
Societal influence operates through public interest, political priorities, and national ambition. Climate science, medicine, energy research, and cosmology receive substantial support because they address collective fears or inspire public imagination. This creates a bias toward fields that produce visible or emotionally resonant results, while slow, foundational inquiries struggle for attention.
The Apollo programme offers a clear example of societal prestige driving scientific and technological priority. It was not only a technical enterprise but a national performance of capability, ambition, and ideological confidence. Space science benefited because it became a symbol of collective achievement. The same logic still appears when cosmology, space telescopes, and planetary missions attract support partly because they speak to public imagination as much as to technical necessity.
2.3 Universities: Incentive and Survival
Universities shape inquiry through career structures and institutional incentives. Publish or perish dynamics, grant dependence, and departmental identity encourage conformity to established frameworks. Safe, fashionable, and grant friendly topics thrive, while long-term, foundational, or dissenting research faces structural resistance. Intellectual orthodoxy emerges not from belief but from institutional survival.
A young researcher entering a field already organised around an established framework may quickly learn that intellectual independence has a price. Grant applications, postdoctoral appointments, conference invitations, and journal refereeing often reward work that extends a recognised programme rather than work that challenges its foundations. The pressure is rarely explicit censorship. It is more subtle: risky questions become career risks, while safe questions become evidence of professional seriousness.
3. How Power Structures Shape Scientific Fields
The influence of these forces is visible across major domains of physics.
3.1 Cosmology
Cosmology thrives because it aligns with societal imagination and national prestige. Large-scale projects such as the Hubble Space Telescope, the James Webb Space Telescope, the Planck satellite, and major galaxy surveys have drawn support because they promise not only technical data but a public story about origins, destiny, and humanity's place in the universe. Speculative frameworks—including inflation and multiverse models—persist partly because they fit this narrative of cosmic grandeur, even when empirical constraints remain limited.
3.2 Particle Physics
Particle physics was historically shaped by military and national priorities during the Cold War. Nuclear weapons research, accelerator laboratories, and state-backed competition over fundamental particles gave high-energy physics unusual prestige and institutional scale. When the Superconducting Super Collider was cancelled in the United States in 1993, it exposed how vulnerable the field had become to political will and public justification. Theoretical frameworks such as supersymmetry and string theory remain influential despite limited empirical support, sustained by institutional momentum as well as by the hope of future experimental confirmation.
3.3 Quantum Foundations
Quantum foundations receive less support because they often lack immediate military utility, societal urgency, and academic safety. Foundational questions—such as the measurement problem, nonlocality, or the completeness of quantum mechanics—remained marginal for decades despite their importance. The history of Bell's theorem is instructive: what once appeared to be a philosophical dispute about hidden variables eventually led to experiments testing quantum nonlocality and, later, to technologies connected with quantum information. Inquiry persisted in small, isolated pockets before parts of it became respectable again.
4. Rebellion Within Scientific Systems
Scientific rebellion today is subtle. It consists of researchers who pursue questions outside institutional priorities, challenge dominant frameworks, or resist the pressure to conform. These individuals often face professional isolation, limited funding, and reduced visibility. Rebellion is essential for progress yet structurally discouraged.
Rebels in cosmology question inflation or dark energy; rebels in particle physics challenge supersymmetry or high energy expectations; rebels in quantum foundations refuse to treat interpretation as settled. Their work highlights the tension between intellectual courage and institutional constraint.
History shows that rebellion is not a romantic exception but a recurring source of progress. Wegener's continental drift was dismissed for decades before plate tectonics transformed geology. Einstein's early work challenged fixed assumptions about space, time, and light. Bell's theorem reopened questions that many physicists had treated as philosophical distractions. These cases do not prove that every dissenter is right; they show that science becomes weaker when dissent is filtered out before evidence has had time to speak.
5. Revolution in a System Resistant to Change
Scientific revolutions occur when evidence becomes undeniable, when technology advances sufficiently, or when younger generations adopt new frameworks. However, revolutions can fail when theories are too entrenched, when evidence remains inaccessible, or when institutional structures resist foundational change.
Revolutions are not purely scientific events; they are institutional negotiations. The success or failure of a paradigm shift depends as much on structural conditions as on empirical data.
The replacement of the geocentric cosmos, the rise of thermodynamics, the acceptance of relativity, and the emergence of plate tectonics all show that scientific revolutions require more than a correct idea. They require instruments, institutions, younger researchers, and a changing sense of what counts as a serious explanation. Evidence matters most, but evidence must still travel through human systems before it can overturn an established worldview.
6. The Economics of Failure
Certain theories in physics have become structurally indispensable. Their failure would destabilize entire research infrastructures. As a result, they are protected through intellectual “bailouts”: supersymmetric particles are moved to higher energies after non-detection, dark matter searches are extended after repeated null results, inflation is reshaped into many versions with different predictions, and string theory is defended through mathematical richness even where direct experimental tests remain unavailable.
Examples include dark matter, inflation, supersymmetry, and string theory. These frameworks support major funding streams, departmental identities, and collaborative structures. Their survival is often determined by institutional necessity rather than empirical validation.
When theories become too important to fail, they also become too important to test. This creates a paradox in which progress slows, inquiry narrows, and orthodoxy hardens.
7. The Future of Scientific Freedom
Scientific freedom requires structural reform. Inquiry must be protected from economic dependence, institutional orthodoxy, and intellectual conformity. A healthier ecosystem would allow theories to fail safely, support long-term foundational research, encourage dissent, and diversify funding sources.
Pluralism is essential. Multiple frameworks must coexist, independent institutes must be empowered, and cross-disciplinary inquiry must be encouraged. Technological advances will expand the empirical reach of physics, enabling tests of previously inaccessible regimes. Cultural renewal is equally necessary: humility, curiosity, and intellectual courage must replace conformity and institutional preservation.
8. A Manifesto for Scientific Renewal
Scientific renewal requires a commitment to principles that restore inquiry to its foundational purpose.
1.	Freedom to Fail. Theories must be allowed to fail without institutional collapse.
2.	Freedom to Dissent. Dissent must be recognized as essential to scientific progress.
3.	Freedom from Economic Dependence Inquiry must not be dictated by military utility, political urgency, or academic fashion.
4.	Freedom from Institutional Orthodoxy No theory should become infrastructure; no framework should define the limits of thought.
5.	Freedom to Ask Forbidden Questions Foundational challenges must be welcomed rather than discouraged.
6.	Freedom Through Pluralism Diverse approaches strengthen scientific resilience.
7.	Freedom to Evolve Theories must adapt to evidence, and institutions must adapt to discovery.
8.	Freedom to Serve Truth Above All Else Evidence must outrank authority, tradition, and convenience.
Scientific renewal begins when truth becomes the only master.
9. Conclusion
Modern physics stands at a crossroads. Its achievements are extraordinary, yet its structures risk constraining the very inquiry that made those achievements possible. The forces that shape scientific research—military priorities, societal expectations, and academic incentives—must be balanced by systems that protect curiosity, dissent, and foundational exploration.
The future of scientific freedom depends on creating an ecosystem where theories can fail safely, where forbidden questions can be asked openly, and where truth is allowed to emerge without institutional constraint. Renewal is not guaranteed, but it is possible. It begins with a commitment to the principles outlined in this paper and with the recognition that science must remain a living, evolving pursuit of understanding.
